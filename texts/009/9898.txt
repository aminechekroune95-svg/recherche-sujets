Document vidéo
Titre : Environmental Racism in the U.S.A.
Source : CBS News, July 18, 2020

Texte
A group of five men and three women are seated cross-legged in a small
circle, in the shade of a flat-roofed brown building in Whiteclay, Nebraska. They are
drinking 24-ounce cans of malt liquor. Three other men are passed out on the
sidewalk, one alone and two lying next to each other. […] Abandoned cars lie junked
behind the buildings. Garbage, empty beer cans, and plastic containers litter the
ground.
Whiteclay, an unincorporated1 village that exists for only a block and a half
before vanishing into the flatlands of the surrounding prairie, has only five or six
permanent residents. It exists to sell beer and malt liquor. It has no town hall, no fire
department, no police department, no garbage collection, no municipal water, no
town sewer system, no parks, no benches, no public restrooms, no schools, no
church, no ambulance service, no civic organizations, and no library. The main
street, Nebraska Highway 87, is the only street in Whiteclay. A few of the buildings
on the street, many of which once served as saloons, are boarded up and padlocked
shut. Several are wooden, with high false fronts and sloping porches that extend over
the dirt sidewalks. […] The eighty percent unemployment on the reservation; the
racism by the neighboring white ranchers and law enforcement; the frequent lack of
running water and electricity in overcrowded trailer homes, and the horrific violence
are numbed or forgotten in drunkenness. Whiteclay is the modern-day version of the
old Indian agencies. It is where Indians surrender. It is where they hand over their
self-esteem and autonomy and wait passively in line for a bag of flour, a piece of lard,
a few blankets.
Verlyn Long Wolf is sixty-two, with silver wire-frame glasses and salt-andpepper hair pulled back into a ponytail. She works at a drop-in center for the
homeless in Rapid City. Most of her life, including her childhood, has been colored by
alcoholism, and the verbal, physical, and sexual abuse that drunkenness brings with
it. When she was a child, she and her brothers and sisters, terrified of the alcoholic
rages of their parents, dug a small cave overlooking a creek near where they lived.
The children would flee to the hiding spot when the parents drank heavily. […] The
family, impoverished and in need of work, traveled to ranches and farms where her
father could find seasonal employment. They camped out in tents or lived out of their
car. Long Wolf did not have a permanent place to live until she was in eighth grade.
As Indians in rural, white farm towns, the family endured racist taunts and abuse.
Chris Hedges and Joe Sacco, Days of Destruction, Days of Revolt, 2012
